### Style source (what we're borrowing from Kurzgesagt and why)

| Kurzgesagt pattern | How this script uses it |
|---|---|
| Viewer is the protagonist ("Imagine you…") | Cold open puts YOU in the $108M career; the Cliff and the Ledger turn the question on YOUR paycheck |
| Counterintuitive hook in the first 15s | "Imagine earning $108 million… then filing for bankruptcy two years later" |
| One big invisible force via a physical metaphor | Ep01's bathtub, now draining: tap = income, drain = spending, 30 extra taps = 30 passengers |
| Escalating scale / honest arithmetic | $108M → ~$53M after tax (his figure) → $12.7M debts vs $4.3M assets (court) |
| A twist that complicates the neat story | He did the "responsible" thing — invested — and that was the anchor |
| Callback continuity | Ep01's janitor, bathtub and 95-cell petri dish return as the mirror image |
| Optimistic ending | His recovery and teaching work is the payoff, then YOUR runway |
| Playful side-gags in visuals, never in the facts | Penny gags live in the VISUAL directions only; never at Walker's expense |

## PART 1 — "The Life" (0:40–2:50)

**[VISUAL: dusk on a city playground court; a lanky teenager shooting alone under a streetlight
(name card drawn locally: ANTOINE WALKER). Then a blue-and-white college arena, a net being cut
down, a draft-night handshake — no logos. A contract glows gold as it's signed.]**

> **VO:** This is Antoine Walker. He grows up on Chicago's South Side, and from early on, basketball looks like his way forward. And he takes it at full speed. At nineteen, he wins a college national championship, then turns professional that same year. *(B05–B06)*
>
> He's picked sixth overall in the draft. Soon after, he signs a six-year contract worth seventy-one million dollars. Pause on that. At an age when most people are negotiating their very first salary, he's already a multimillionaire, many times over. *(B07–B08)*
>
> A three-time All-Star, and later an NBA champion. Over twelve seasons, he has said, he earned about one hundred and eight million. Years later, he put it in one sentence: the money came so fast without the education. Hold on to that line. *(B09–B10)*

**[VISUAL: a boulevard of generic silhouetted music-star billboards; then a cheerful museum of
pedestals — car, jewelry, suit, a table for twenty — while the drain grate under the floor quietly
widens. Tender beat: he hands his mother the key to a family home. A paycheck drawn as a little
bus; thirty friendly passengers climb aboard; Penny counts them on her fingers, ears rising in surprise.]**

> **VO:** So he lives like the money will never stop. He has said he modeled his lifestyle on the music moguls he admired. Cars. Jewelry. Tailored suits. Big nights out. Your eyes see a celebration. The math sees a drain opening wider and wider. *(B11–B12)*
>
> Here's the part people skip. Much of that money went to others. One of his first buys, he's said: his mother's house. By his own estimate, at the peak, he was supporting around thirty people. One paycheck. Thirty passengers. Every single month. *(B13–B14)*

**[VISUAL: calendar pages flip from 2008 to 2010 (drawn); a courthouse; a balance scale tips —
the red DEBTS pan sinks, the gold ASSETS pan rises (figures drawn locally). A championship ring
lifted off a velvet cushion. Penny rolls up her sleeves and picks up a screwdriver.]**

> **VO:** His last NBA game comes in 2008. Then, in May 2010, he files for Chapter 7 bankruptcy. That's about two years later. The court papers list twelve point seven million dollars in debts, against just four point three million in assets. *(B15–B16)*
>
> Even his championship ring is sold to help repay his creditors. So how does this happen? Let's take it apart. *(B17)*

## PART 2 — "The Evidence" (2:50–7:20)

Three leaks, in the order they hit the bathtub.

### Leak 1 — "The Burn" (2:50–4:20)

**[VISUAL: Ep01's bathtub returns. Penny lowers a measuring tape into a gold-plated tub that turns
out to be shallow. A tall gold bar splits: half slides into a grey government building (drawn
start frame: $108M EARNED / $55.2M TAXES).]**

> **VO:** Remember the bathtub from last time? Income pours in through the tap. Spending drains out the bottom. Wealth is only what stays in the tub. So let's measure his tub, starting with the number almost nobody mentions. *(B18–B19)*
>
> Taxes. Of the one hundred and eight million, he has said fifty-five million went straight to the taxman. That leaves something closer to fifty-three million. Still enormous. But the real number is only about half the headline. *(B20–B21)*

**[VISUAL: a parade of cars, jewelry and casino chips flushing out of the drain; blank checks
bouncing across a casino floor (no writing on them); thirty small taps pulling water from the
side of the tub while Penny tries to plug two with her paws. Two tubs side by side — the gold tub
visibly EMPTY, water blasting straight through it (fixes the Ep01 two-tubs contradiction).]**

> **VO:** Now the drain. Cars that lose value the day you drive them home. Jewelry. Gambling. All of it flowing out. And, according to court records, more than a million dollars in bad checks written to casinos. That's the drain at full speed. *(B22–B23)*
>
> Plus thirty people drawing on one income. Those costs don't happen once. They repeat. Every month. Every year. Here's what your eyes get wrong. His tap was a firehose. But his drain was nearly as wide as the tap. *(B24–B25)*
>
> Sometimes wider. Which means a bigger paycheck doesn't automatically make you richer. Only a bigger gap between in and out does. *(B26)*

**[ON-SCREEN stamp (drawn): "A BIGGER PAYCHECK IS NOT A BIGGER GAP."]**

### Leak 2 — "The Empire" (4:20–6:00)

**[VISUAL: Penny ticks a box on a report card with a green pen. An architect's model of city houses and
half-built blocks; deeds stack around it (drawn: $18M). Loan papers signed in glowing gold, each
signature chained to his wrist. Three coloured blocks — concentrated, leveraged, illiquid — stack
into a wobbling tower.]**

> **VO:** Leak number two is the one that should unsettle you most, because on paper, he did the responsible thing. He invested. He has said he put around eighteen million dollars into real estate, much of it back home in his city, Chicago. *(B27–B28)*
>
> Properties. Development projects. Loans. And, as he has explained, he signed as the personal guarantor. His own name on the debt. Let's name the three risks stacked on top of each other here. First: concentrated. One kind of asset, in one city. *(B29–B30)*
>
> Second: leveraged, meaning built with borrowed money. Third: illiquid. You can't sell a half-finished building in an afternoon. *(B31)*

**[VISUAL: a red line marked 2008 sweeps across the model like a cold front; everything freezes.
Bank buildings reach out with open palms. A gold anchor drops into deep water, dragging the car,
the jewelry and the coin stack down with it. Callback: Ep01's 95-cell petri dish — one cell pops,
the rest keep glowing. A single glass sphere shatters beside a 95-tile mosaic that loses one tile
and stays whole.]**

> **VO:** Then 2008 arrives. The housing market doesn't just fall. It freezes. Buyers vanish, and nothing can be sold. But the loans still have to be repaid. In his words, the banks wanted their money back. They didn't want empty lots. *(B32–B33)*
>
> The eighteen-million-dollar bet stops being an asset and becomes an anchor, dragging everything else down with it. Remember the janitor? He owned small pieces of ninety-five companies. When one of them went to zero, the rest kept going. *(B34–B35)*
>
> This was one big piece of one thing. Diversification isn't decoration. It's how you make every mistake survivable. *(B36)*

### Leak 3 — "The Cliff" (6:00–7:20)

**[VISUAL: Penny turns and points at you. A gentle hill-shaped income line (a normal career) next
to a tall narrow tower (a pro career) that ends at a sheer cliff; the gold income line stops, the
red spending line keeps running out over thin air, carrying the bus of passengers. A pocket watch
spins; an airplane rolls down a runway whose far end is falling away.]**

> **VO:** Leak number three is the one that applies to you, even if your paycheck has never had six zeros on it. Most careers taper. You earn more, then maybe a little less, then you retire. A pro athlete's career doesn't taper. *(B37–B38)*
>
> It's a whole working life squeezed into roughly a decade. And then the income doesn't slow down. It simply stops. His stops in 2008, when he's thirty-one. But the mortgages, the lifestyle, the thirty passengers? Those keep going. *(B39–B40)*
>
> From his final game to his bankruptcy filing takes roughly two years. And that gap in time is the real measurement. Not what he earned. How long the life he'd built could keep running with no new income coming in. *(B41–B42)*
>
> Two years. On a career that paid him over a hundred million dollars. That's how fast the drain wins without the tap. Which leaves you with one uncomfortable, simple question. If your paycheck stopped tomorrow, how many months could you last? *(B43–B44)*

**[VISUAL: the plane stops at the edge; drawn panel: RUNWAY: ~24 MONTHS. First-person: your own
paycheck fades in your hands while a blank runway gauge waits on the desk.]**

## PART 3 — "The Comeback" (the verdict) (7:20–8:40)

**[VISUAL: dawn breaks over the skyline; Penny smiles and puts her glasses back on. An older Walker
walks up a winding path, each step lighting gold. He opens the dusty book from earlier and light
pours out. A bright classroom: he stands beside a whiteboard bathtub sketch while young athletes
lean in. He hands the glowing book on; the light passes down a long line of people.]**

> **VO:** That could be the end of the story. But it isn't. And honestly, the next part is the one that matters most. He doesn't disappear. He works his way through the bankruptcy, one step at a time, and he comes out the other side. *(B45–B46)*
>
> Then he does something rarer than earning a hundred million dollars. He turns the loss itself into a lesson. Today he works in financial education, telling this exact story to young athletes, so that their numbers end differently. *(B47–B48)*
>
> He paid a fortune for this lesson. Now he shares it openly, with anyone willing to listen, so others don't pay it. *(B49)*

**[VISUAL: seasons cycle in time-lapse — sun, autumn, first snow. Two houses in the snow: the
janitor's tiny cabin with a warm window and a full woodpile; a dark mansion with an empty
woodshed. The janitor waves from his door.]**

> **VO:** So what's the takeaway? Income is not wealth. Income is weather. It can stay sunny for years, then turn. Wealth is whatever you've built by the time the weather changes. The janitor proved a small income can build a fortune. *(B50–B51)*
>
> This story proves a fortune can't survive without the same habits. Same math. Opposite direction. Build for winter. *(B52)*

**[ON-SCREEN stamp (drawn): "INCOME IS A SEASON. BUILD FOR WINTER." Penny wraps a mint scarf
around her neck.]**

## THE LEDGER — "Your Runway" (8:40–10:20)

**[VISUAL: aesthetic shift — the animated world swooshes into a stylized flat-vector rendition of
the Ledger **Runway** calculator (`calculator.html#runway`: savings ÷ monthly spending, gold bar,

**[MUSIC: lifts — resolution, not hype.]**

> **VO:** So let's stop talking about his numbers, and run yours. There's a name for what we've been measuring. It's called runway. Runway is simple. What you have saved, divided by what you spend each month. The same equation works at every income. *(B53–B54)*
>
> Say you spend five thousand a month and have ten thousand saved. Divide one by the other. That's a two-month runway. With thirty thousand saved, it's six months. That's around the minimum many financial planners like to see. *(B55–B56)*
>
> With three hundred thousand invested, it's five years. And suddenly, the weather starts losing its power over you. Notice the trick. Every dollar you stop spending works twice. It grows your savings, and it shrinks the number they're divided by. *(B57–B58)*

**[VISUAL: a first-person hand taps the blank calculator. Penny hops over to stand beside the
Wealth Garden App Store badge — she IS the app mascot, so the hand-off is seamless.]**

> **VO:** The free runway calculator is linked below. Put in your own real numbers, and find out how many months you'd last. And to watch your runway grow, tracked across every account, our Wealth Garden app is on the App Store, also linked below. *(B59–B60)*
>
> None of this is financial advice. It's arithmetic, and one man's very public story, told in his own words. Next time: no athletes, no millionaires. Just your paycheck, and where it actually goes. Every fortune leaves a paper trail. *(B61–B62)*

### Flagged for Lawrence before recording

1. **Debt-free date.** The original script says he "announced he was debt-free" in 2013. The
   sources found only show the bankruptcy **discharged in March 2012** (TMZ, 2012). The VO avoids
   a date ("he works his way through… and comes out the other side"). If a 2013 interview is
   found, a date can go back in.
2. **When the $71M deal was signed.** Reported as 1997 in some places and 1999 in others
   (Yahoo Sports). The VO says "soon after" the draft, which is true either way.
3. **Casino checks.** "More than a million dollars in bad checks" is how it's widely reported;
   ESPN (2010) reports he agreed to pay **over $900,000** to settle the charges in 2009. The VO
   keeps the original "according to court records, more than a million dollars"; worth
   confirming against the court record, or softening to "around a million dollars".
4. **"Around thirty people".** His own estimate (CNN Money, via NBC Sports). Some reports quote
   his mother's estimate of ~70. The VO uses his figure, attributed to him.
5. **Two new sources** (both his own interviews) have been added to back lines that were already
   in the script: the personal-guarantor detail and "the banks wanted their money back"
   (ESPN *Sessions*, 2014), the first-contract purchases incl. his mother's house (ESPN *Sessions*; Yahoo Sports), and the ring sale (ESPN 2010 / TMZ 2012).
